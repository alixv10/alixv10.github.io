Final reflection

Whose rights, and whose representation?

Have you noticed the tension between protecting the rights of individuals and organizations to control how their data is used, and ensuring that AI systems represent a broad range of people and perspectives?

Individuals and organizations have legitimate reasons to withhold their information or creative work from AI training. Respecting those choices is important. However, when data is withheld or excluded, the resulting datasets may become less representative of the people and perspectives an AI system is meant to serve.

At the same time, simply adding more data does not automatically reduce bias or prevent misrepresentation. The way data is selected, measured, and interpreted matters just as much as its quantity.

This leaves us with a new and difficult question: how can we respect people's rights to control their data while also working toward AI systems that represent society more fairly?

Putting it into practice

What happens when we give AI more information?

To explore this further, these articles were used in UBC's HelpMe Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system. Unlike a general AI model that may draw on information from its broader training, a RAG system retrieves information from a defined collection of documents, or corpus, to help generate its response.

In my first attempt, the system struggled to answer my question using the articles available in its original corpus. I then expanded the corpus by curating and adding the two articles by Obermeyer et al. and Buick which I selected for their relevance to my research question.

The second attempt produced a different result. Although the system had access to more articles, it retrieved only one: an article I had deliberately included because of its connection to my question. However, rather than addressing the withholding of data from data sets used to train AI models, the response focused on companies intentionally withholding information about their training datasets, potentially to protect themselves from copyright infringement or for other reasons.

The system had found a relevant source, but it appeared to interpret my question differently from how I intended it. Adding more information improved the opportunity to retrieve a relevant article, but it did not guarantee that the system would understand the broader issue I was investigating and correctly answer my question.

What does this tell us?

This experiment highlights another limitation of simply increasing the amount of information available to an AI system. The composition of a corpus matters, but so do the system's retrieval choices and its interpretation of the user's question. Even carefully curated information can be used in unexpected ways or lead to an answer that misses the intended point.

Ultimately, more information does not automatically produce more accurate, balanced, or representative answers. We must also consider which sources are selected, which are retrieved, and how the system interprets and uses them.

Important distinction

Adding articles to the course corpus and making them available for retrieval by HelpMe is not the same as changing the training data used to develop an AI model. The articles were made available as reference material for the RAG system to retrieve, rather than being used to retrain or modify the underlying model.

Nevertheless, this exploration was useful in highlighting limitations that remain even in RAG systems with a bounded collection of information to work from. Restricting the sources available to a model does not guarantee that it will retrieve the most relevant information, interpret it as intended, or produce a response that adequately addresses the question.
